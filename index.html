Growth Playbook

Media Buying
Handbook

You already know how to make great creative, build funnels and close deals. This is the other half: how to buy, track and scale on Meta without burning money learning it the hard way.

Everything here comes from real accounts and real mistakes, ours and other people's. No theory that hasn't paid rent.

20 slides · print to PDF friendly
01
Foundations

How Meta's algorithm actually works

Here's the mental shift: you're not buying ads. You're renting an optimization machine, and it's only as smart as the data you feed it.

  • Every impression is an auction. Meta shows your ad to whoever it predicts will fire your conversion event. That prediction is the whole product.
  • To make good predictions, an ad set needs about 50 conversions a week. Under that, it sits in "Learning limited": delivery gets erratic, CPMs creep up, and the results you see mean very little.
  • Signal pools per ad set. Three ad sets at $200/day each learn three times slower than one at $600/day. Same money. Worse outcome. This surprises everyone the first time.
  • And the event you optimize for decides everything downstream. Too rare and the machine starves. Too shallow and it happily fills your CRM with garbage. There's a whole slide on this later.
If you remember one thing

Signal volume beats neat organization. Every extra ad set or campaign you create "to keep things tidy" quietly taxes the machine's ability to learn.

02
SOP · Account structure

Keep the account boring and flat

Campaign = one funnel→Ad set = one audience→Ads = the ideas being tested
  • One campaign per funnel. Don't mix two funnels in the same ad set hoping Meta balances them, it won't. It dumps all the spend on whichever gets the cheaper conversion and quietly starves the other.
  • One or two ad sets per campaign. That's it. Broad targeting by default. You open a new ad set when the audience or the funnel genuinely changes, not to organize your creatives.
  • Default habit: a new ad goes into an existing ad set. Boring, and correct.
  • Run a testing campaign on a capped budget. When something proves itself there, promote it to the core campaign and put real money behind it.
  • Don't bother with fancy targeting for B2B, Meta can't reliably find "companies over $3M revenue" anyway. Your copy does the targeting. More on that in two slides.
What bad looks like

40 ad sets, most of them paused, the live ones splitting $50–200/day each, everything flagged Learning limited. You can't judge any creative inside a structure like that. Consolidate first, evaluate after.

03
SOP · Creative testing

One ad, one argument

The unit of testing isn't "an ad", it's a numbered concept: one angle, one argument, expressed as a handful of hooks and a single body.

  • One value proposition per ad. When an ad argues three things at once, it argues nothing. If you have three arguments, that's three concepts.
  • Give every concept an ID and never break the pattern: AC_017_FUNNEL_Concept Name. That same ID goes into the UTMs, the reports and the creative library, so six months from now you can trace revenue back to the exact idea that produced it.
  • Per concept: around 5 hooks for static, 3 for video, one body that works for both. The hooks are the variable. The argument is the constant.
  • Log everything. Winners hand you their exact wording for the next round. Losers tell you which arguments the market already rejected, and that's worth almost as much.
The hook test · ask it before and after writing

"This runs broad. The copy is the only targeting I have. Would only my ideal customer recognize themselves in this hook, or could anyone nod along?" If anyone could nod along, it's not done.

04
SOP · Launch mechanics

Launching a test wave, step by step

  • Launch in waves of ~5 concepts. Daily budget = monthly budget ÷ 30, and match the wave size to the budget: on $100/day, five ads is already plenty. Don't crowd the wave.
  • Give every ad set a $5/day minimum spend so campaign budget optimization can't starve the new ones before they get a fair shot. Then let it run 3–5 days, make sure everything is actually spending, and turn off what isn't working.
  • Found a low-spend ad with a great CPL? Nudge the minimum up gently, $5 → 10 → 15 → 20. Jump it straight to $30 and the performer usually dies. Nobody knows exactly why. It just does.
  • Scale the minimum to the CPL, too: a $20 floor is plenty when leads cost $2, and useless when a conversion costs $60, it can't even buy you one event.
  • Turn on dynamic creative so variations get tested inside the ad, and before anything goes live, check the conversion event shows green in Events Manager (fired recently, actively tracking).
  • After duplicating an ad, stay on the page until it shows "in review" or "active". Duplicates sometimes throw a silent error and sit there dead until someone notices, days later.
Budget floor sanity check

Your daily budget should buy at least 5 conversions a day across the wave, 10 is healthier. If it can't, the wave is too big or the budget is too small. Fix one of them.

05
SOP · Copy

Copy rules we learned the expensive way

Steal the customer's words, don't write your own

The best hooks are barely written, they're lifted. Go where your buyers complain (forums, sales calls, email replies) and use the closest thing to their exact phrasing. The moment you "improve" it with dramatic wording, it starts smelling like an ad. If the raw material isn't visceral enough, the answer is more research, not more imagination.

A body that converts, in order

Poke the pain (1–2 lines)→Show the mechanism→Prove it, specifically→One ask

Say who it's NOT for

If the offer has a floor, minimum budget, company size, put it in the ad. Sounds counterintuitive, but a disqualifier is a filter you'd otherwise pay for in wasted clicks. And only promise what you actually control.

Words that are banned

"Game-changer", "unlock", "transform", "revolutionize", anything where an adjective is doing the argument's job. Also: polished, brand-perfect creative usually loses to something that looks like a person made it. Every team resists this until their own data says otherwise.

Formatting that quietly matters

Headlines under 50 characters, descriptions under ~120–150, past that, Meta truncates and your best line dies behind a "see more". And match the CTA button to the funnel: "Subscribe" for newsletters, "Download" for lead magnets, "Sign Up" for qualification flows.

06
Framework · Metrics

Two numbers run everything, in this order

Every funnel has a volume number (what a lead costs) and a quality number (what a real lead costs, qualified, booked, verified). You need both to rank creatives, and they're not equals.

MetricJobWeight
Volume CAC (cost per lead)The primary ranking. Can this creative acquire at target cost?~70%
Quality CAC (cost per qualified lead / booked call)The tiebreaker and the veto. Are these leads real people we want?~30%
  • Quality CAC running 20–30% over target is tolerable, but only while volume CAC is on target.
  • CTR, CPM, CVR are for diagnosis, not decisions. CTR especially is noisy at weekly volumes, read it for insight, never rank by it.
  • And don't rank anything with fewer than ~5 quality conversions. Two data points isn't a ranking, it's a coin flip.
07
SOP · Scaling decisions

When to scale, hold, or kill

What you're seeingWhat you do
Volume CAC on target, quality within ~130% of targetScale it
Volume on target, quality way offReduce, then figure out why the leads are bad
Volume above target but quality is exceptionalHold. Don't scale. Say the tradeoff out loud in the report
Volume above targetCut or hold. Never scale. No exceptions
The rule people try to argue with

Never scale a creative that's above target on volume CAC, no matter how beautiful its quality numbers look. Scale it and you drag the whole account's average further from target. It feels wrong. The math doesn't care.

Breaking ties (when volume CAC is within ~10%)

  • Both above target? Budget goes to the one with clearly better quality CAC (30%+ lower) and better CTR.
  • Both on target, nearly identical? Don't chase an 8% volume edge when its quality cost is double. Quality wins the tie.

Pause vs reduce

One bad week means nothing, every creative has them. Look at 3–4 weeks plus all-time. A sustained pattern earns a pause. Anything shorter earns a budget reduction and another look next week.

08
SOP · Budget

Pacing: a weekly ritual, not a vibe

  1. Confirm the monthly budget at the start of every month. Budgets change more often than anyone remembers to mention.
  2. Once a week, do the honest math: "Day 12 of 31, so we should be 38.7% through the budget. Are we?"
  3. Every account gets a label: over pace, under pace, on pace.
  4. The fix is arithmetic, not intuition: remaining budget ÷ days left, compare to the current daily. Over → trim. Under → add, but only behind creatives that have already proven themselves. On pace → say so and touch nothing.
How to actually raise budgets

Steps of 20–30% every few days. Doubling overnight resets learning and you pay for it in a week of chaos. And scale by adding budget to the winning ad set, not by duplicating it, clones split your own signal and end up bidding against each other.

09
SOP · Daily routine

The daily check, in ten minutes

  • Open the month-to-date view, all campaigns. Last month is done, you can't fix it, so don't stare at it.
  • Compare yesterday against the month-to-date baseline. That's the whole trick. An ad set that runs $30 CPL all month and printed $70 yesterday is telling you something.
  • When one goes sideways: cut its budget (say $700/day → $400), let it earn its way back, then restore. And reallocate the difference the same day, to whichever ad set has been boringly consistent all month. The daily total gets spent either way; you choose where.
  • Once a week, scan the week-over-week deltas on CPM, CTR, conversion rate and cost per result. A conversion rate that fell from 29% to 8% has a cause. Drill from account to campaign to ad until you find where it lives. You won't always find the answer, but you'll always find where to look next.
  • For ads where quality data arrives late (sales, activations, call outcomes): cap their spend (say $30/day) until the quality verdict is in. Don't let an unproven ad spend big just because its CPL looks pretty.
10
Craft · Audience plays

Staying broad without flying blind

  • Exclude past converters properly. Uploading the email list barely works, the email someone subscribes with rarely matches the one on their decade-old Facebook account. Build a website custom audience of everyone who converted in the last 180 days and exclude that. Meta knows exactly who they are.
  • Use breakdowns to discover the real ICP. Break results down by age, gender, day. Sometimes the client's assumed customer and the converting customer are different people, that's a finding worth bringing to them. Look at conversion rate, not just volume, and don't read anything into $200 of spend.
  • Bias with value rules, don't narrow the targeting. If the 45–54 bracket converts best, don't restrict the ad set to it. Set a value rule: "bid 20% more for 45–54". You keep the broad setup that works and still push budget where the data points. Works for gender, days, even hours.
  • Time retargeting to the funnel's natural rhythm. If subscribers typically buy the follow-up offer around week three, retarget them in that window with that offer, not on day one with a generic reminder.
  • Watch frequency for fatigue. Above ~2 on active ads (measured since launch, per ad set) means people are seeing it too often. Pair it with CTR: frequency climbing while CTR falls is the fatigue signature.
11
Tracking · Pixel & events

Pixel, CAPI, and picking the right event

  • Run both trackers: the pixel in the browser and the Conversions API from the server, deduplicated. Browsers block more tracking every year; CAPI catches what the pixel drops. Most tools (HubSpot, Zapier, most ESPs) do this without custom code.
  • Map the whole journey as events: page view, content engagement, quiz start, quiz complete, lead, booked call. You'll optimize on one but you want eyes on all of them.
  • Then verify what each event actually fires on. True story: a report once used the "registration" event as the lead count and showed a $9.01 CPL. The real lead event said $3.27. Same account, same week, one wrong assumption about an event name.
  • And if a live account shows zero conversions, that's a tracking bug until you've proven otherwise. Check the pixel before concluding the ads don't work.
Picking the optimization event

Go as deep in the funnel as you can while still clearing ~50 events a week per ad set. Reality check: a booked call costs around $300 on average, so below roughly $10k/month in spend, a call-optimized funnel simply never gets enough events to learn. Optimize on the lead and feed call quality back through the CRM, next slide.

12
Tracking · CRM loop

Teach Meta what a good lead looks like

The endgame: Meta optimizing for leads that become revenue, without you switching to an event too rare to learn from. That's what the CRM feedback loop is for.

  1. Connect the CRM to Meta. HubSpot does this natively: Marketing → Ads → connect the account, turn on CRM event syncing. An hour of setup, once.
  2. Keep lifecycle stages honest: lead → qualified → call booked → won. This progression is literally the training data. Sloppy stages, sloppy optimization.
  3. Once a few hundred synced leads have moved through stages, switch campaigns to Conversion Leads. Meta still optimizes on lead volume, but starts favoring the profiles that historically progress.
  4. Set expectations: this pays off as data accrues, not in week one. Which is exactly the argument for connecting it early.

Same idea works for newsletters: feed subscriber quality (opens, verified status) back per concept, and you learn which angle brings readers and which brings dead emails.

13
Tracking · UTMs

UTMs: the attribution spine

Meta reports what Meta saw. Your CRM reports what actually happened. UTMs are the only thing joining the two, and they only work if nobody freelances the schema.

ParameterWhat goes in itWhy
utm_sourceFixed per channel: facebookadsSplits paid from organic everywhere downstream
utm_mediumAd set nameAudience-level rollups
utm_campaignConcept ID (AC_017_...)Revenue per angle, this feeds the concept library
utm_contentMeta ad ID ({{ad.id}})Unambiguous join back to the exact ad
utm_termAd set ID ({{adset.id}})Same thing, ad set level
  • Use Meta's dynamic parameters ({{ad.id}} and friends) so the tagging happens by itself and nobody fat-fingers a link.
  • A broken UTM doesn't error, it just silently loses data. A creative showing spend but zero attributed conversions? Check the link before you blame the ad.
  • Belt and suspenders: also capture the Meta click ID (fbclid) into the CRM. Paid = UTM match or click ID present.
14
Tracking · Attribution

The numbers will disagree. Here's how to referee

  • Meta counts by attribution window (7-day click, 1-day view by default). Your platform counts by UTM at signup. They will never match exactly, and both are "right" by their own rules. Stop expecting them to reconcile to the dollar.
  • Spend, though, always comes from the ad platform. Never trust a composite metric (CPL, cost per qualified lead) from a dashboard you haven't checked against platform spend at least once.
  • Cautionary tale: a dashboard once showed a cost per qualified lead of $15.70 where Meta said $49.98, its spend column was missing whole campaigns and mismatched UTMs had dumped conversions into a legacy bucket. It even recommended killing the one creative driving every qualified lead that week. The source of truth (platform spend ÷ CRM-scored leads) told the opposite story.
  • So: before any kill-or-scale decision based on a dashboard, reconcile it against Meta once. If the gap is big, the dashboard is what's broken.
Reflex to build

Zero conversions in one system ≠ dead creative. Check the UTM, check the event, check the window. Most "dead" creatives turn out to be tracking bugs wearing a costume.

15
Downstream · Lead hygiene

The click is not the finish line

Every ad dollar is wasted if the lead lands in a dirty database or the follow-up email lands in spam. Buying and deliverability are one system, and the second one fails quietly.

  • Validate at the door. Email validation on forms, double opt-in on lists. Gate content without validation and people will type asdf@gmail.com to get the PDF, then you'll mail that address weekly and wonder why Gmail hates you.
  • Never bulk-import cold contacts into a sending platform. Not a purchased list, not a "quick export of all CRM contacts". It's the fastest known way to torch a sending domain, and the recovery takes months of disciplined engaged-only sending.
  • Sunset the non-engagers automatically: no opens or clicks in 90–120 days, out of the sends. Mailbox providers grade you on engagement rate. Dead weight on the list taxes every future email.
  • Authenticate the domain (SPF, DKIM, DMARC with reports on) and watch Google Postmaster. Authentication earns you the right to compete for the inbox. Engagement is what wins it.
  • And nurture only works on people who expected email from you. Consent at capture is a deliverability strategy wearing a compliance costume.
16
SOP · Reporting

Reports produce decisions, not dashboards

Same five blocks, every week, same order:

  1. Budget pacing. Month, day, % through, each account over/under/on pace.
  2. Cost analysis. Volume CAC first, quality CAC second, each marked against target. That order is deliberate, don't flip it.
  3. Top 3 creatives ranked by volume and volume CAC, quality as context, with preview links.
  4. The winning angle. One paragraph on why the winner is winning. The argument, not the asset.
  5. Next steps. The section everything else exists for. Every bullet is a verb: scale, hold, reduce, pause, investigate.
  • Every KPI ships with its week-over-week delta. A number without its delta is trivia.
  • After the report goes out, log the numbers somewhere the next report can find them. Future-you, doing next week's comparison, will be grateful.
  • CPM, CTR and friends stay behind the decision metrics. Always.
17
Judgment · Scope & expectations

What ads can fix, and what they can't

The cleanest mental line: media buying owns everything before the click. After the click, the landing page, the funnel, the offer, the buyer advises, but the funnel's owner decides and executes.

  • Ads cannot manufacture demand for an unproven product. Advertising a thing nobody has ever bought is a different, much harder problem than marketing a thing people already buy, and no amount of creative testing changes that. Know which problem you're solving before you promise results.
  • When a post-click fix is needed, run it through three questions: would it move performance meaningfully? is it cheap or expensive to do? would the fix be reusable, or a one-off? Two or three yeses, do it. Otherwise, recommend it and hand it to whoever owns the funnel.
  • And when someone asks for a tactic you're confident won't work (wrong channel, wrong event, budget too small for the funnel), the useful move isn't a quiet yes. It's showing the math for why, and proposing what would work instead.
A pattern to avoid

Agreeing to optimize for an event the budget can't support, because saying yes felt easier on the call. The campaign starves, everyone's confused in three weeks, and the post-mortem writes itself. Do the event-volume math out loud, in the meeting.

18
Recap

The ten rules worth arguing about

  1. Signal volume beats neat organization. One or two ad sets per campaign.
  2. One ad, one argument. The copy is the targeting.
  3. Optimize on the deepest event that still clears ~50 a week. Feed deeper quality back through the CRM.
  4. Volume CAC decides, quality CAC vetoes. Above-target volume never scales.
  5. One bad week proves nothing. Patterns earn pauses; blips earn reductions.
  6. One UTM schema, dynamic parameters, no freelancing.
  7. Spend comes from the ad platform. Reconcile every dashboard once before trusting it.
  8. Zero conversions is a tracking bug until proven otherwise.
  9. Steal the customer's words. Winners' copy seeds the next round.
  10. Protect the destination: validated capture, double opt-in, engaged-only sending, authenticated domain.
